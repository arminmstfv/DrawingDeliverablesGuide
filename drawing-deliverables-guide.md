# Drawing Deliverables Guide

What gets produced in each phase of a project, what every drawing sheet should show at Schematic Design (SD), Design Development (DD) and Construction Documents (CD), one small tenant suite traced through every phase, and the full list of documents a project can produce.

**Sources:** *NCIDQ Interior Design Reference Manual*, 7th ed. (David Kent Ballast, PPI/Kaplan, 2021), Chs. 4–6, 8, 18, 22–31, summarized and paraphrased for study use; sheet-by-sheet SD/DD/CD lists from a professional drawing-deliverables checklist. Chapter numbers refer to that edition. Code figures follow the IBC and 2010 ADA Standards as the manual presents them; always check the code edition your jurisdiction has adopted.

## 1. What belongs in each phase

Seven phases, from first meeting to post-occupancy. Work done in each phase is presented to the client and approved before the next one starts.

| # | Phase | Abbrev. | Closes when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pre-design & Programming | PR | The client approves the written program, sometimes by signature. Design does not start without that approval. |
| 2 | Schematic Design | SD | The client approves the schematic design before design development begins. |
| 3 | Design Development | DD | The client approves DD in writing. This is the last chance to make changes without hurting cost and schedule. |
| 4 | Construction Documents | CD | The owner approves the final set, which is issued for bidding or negotiation and for permit. |
| 5 | Bidding & Negotiation | BN | Contract award and signed agreements. The contractor then applies for the building permit. |
| 6 | Contract Administration | CA | The work is ready for the substantial-completion inspection. |
| 7 | Closeout & Post-occupancy | CO | Final payment. The designer's formal services can end with the final certificate for payment. |

### 1.1 Pre-design & Programming (PR)

*Pre-design · programming · project setup* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 4, 5, 6, 18, 22, 23, 27, 29

**Purpose:** Define the problem before drawing anything: who the client is, which spaces they need, how big and how connected, under which codes, and for what budget and schedule.

**What you do**

- Research the project type and the client; identify stakeholders and the real decision makers.
- Set scope, fee, schedule and team; sign the owner–designer agreement (AIA B152 or ASID 301–303).
- Select consultants and bring them in early (structural, mechanical, electrical, specialty).
- Survey and measure the existing space, produce base drawings and photograph existing conditions.
- Gather information: interviews, questionnaires, observation, inventories of furniture and equipment, existing space standards.
- Size every space by people, equipment or activity, then add circulation and support space.
- Record adjacencies as mandatory, desirable or unimportant in a matrix and a bubble diagram; add a stacking diagram for multi-floor projects.
- Start a preliminary code checklist: occupancy, area, exits, travel distance, common path, dead ends, accessibility.
- Build a preliminary budget (area × unit cost, as low / medium / high) and estimate the overall schedule.

**What gets produced**

*Drawings & diagrams*

- Measured base plans of existing conditions
- Adjacency matrix and bubble diagrams
- Stacking diagram (multi-story projects only)

*Written*

- Programming report
- Owner–designer agreement with scope by phase
- Preliminary code checklist
- Existing furniture inventory (if reusing furniture)
- Design and production schedule (bar chart or CPM)

*Presentation*

- Programming presentation: summary charts and tables for a small client; a formal report plus boards or slides for a large one

*Cost*

- Preliminary budget from area × cost per square foot (or per unit), in three ranges

**Closes when:** The client approves the written program, sometimes by signature. Design does not start without that approval.

**Exam notes**

- A programming report covers: executive summary, goals, space needs (staff and projections, workflow, tabulation with circulation), adjacencies, code requirements, analysis of the existing space (architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, life safety), budget and schedule, and programming concepts.
- Many contracts leave programming out of basic services; AIA B152 includes it.
- A construction schedule given now is only an estimate; the contractor sets the real one.

### 1.2 Schematic Design (SD)

*Concept · space planning* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 6, 18, 22, 27, 28

**Purpose:** Turn the approved program into a design concept and a space plan the client can react to, with a first statement of probable cost.

**What you do**

- Develop the design concept from the program: theory, circulation, light, color, materials, furniture, accessories.
- Translate bubble diagrams into scaled block diagrams on the base plan, keeping as many adjacencies as possible.
- Lay out preliminary space plans: partitions, circulation, public and private zones, furniture.
- Check basic exiting for each option: number of exits, separation, travel distance, common path, dead-end corridors, door swings.
- Study code decisions with big cost impact now (fire-rated glazing vs. gypsum board, for example).
- Coordinate consultants and building systems; hold the sustainability workshop before SD ends if the project has a sustainable objective.
- Choose broad color and material palettes and preliminary furniture types.
- Prepare the statement of probable project cost and apply value engineering while changes are cheap.

**What gets produced**

*Drawings*

- Concept diagrams on the base plan
- Block diagrams
- Schematic space plan(s), usually with furniture
- Sketches, perspectives and study models

*Written*

- Design concept narrative
- Updated code checklist
- Sustainability plan (if the project has a sustainable objective)

*Presentation*

- Color and material palettes or sample boards
- Preliminary furniture types (images)
- Rendered plan for a formal presentation

*Cost*

- Preliminary statement of probable project cost

**Closes when:** The client approves the schematic design before design development begins.

**Exam notes**

- The designer is not responsible for final project cost; estimates are professional judgment unless a fixed limit of cost is agreed in writing.
- Value engineering is best applied during SD and DD.
- Detailed code requirements can wait for DD unless they affect the plan or the budget.

### 1.3 Design Development (DD)

*Design fixed · products selected* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 6, 9, 18, 20, 21, 22, 27

**Purpose:** Fix the size, scope and character of the project: exact plan dimensions, specific products, finishes, furniture and lighting, with an updated cost.

**What you do**

- Refine the approved schematic: partition and door locations fixed, furniture and fixture layout finalized.
- Develop the lighting design and a preliminary reflected ceiling plan.
- Sketch special built-in cabinetry and custom furniture; develop the key details.
- Select specific materials, finishes, colors and furniture; assemble sample boards.
- Run the detailed code checklist: ratings, glazing, doors and hardware, finish flame spread, toilet accessibility, stairs and ramps, exit signs, extinguishers.
- Check detailed accessibility: route surfaces and thresholds, toilet rooms, grab bars, reach ranges, signage.
- Coordinate HVAC, power, lighting, plumbing and sprinkler layouts with the consultants.
- Write outline specifications where the contract calls for them.
- Update the estimate with the parameter method and make trade-offs to stay on budget.

**What gets produced**

*Drawings*

- Detailed plans: partitions, doors, furniture and fixture layout
- Lighting design / preliminary RCP
- Interior elevations and 3-D views that show the character of the design
- Sketches of special built-ins and custom furniture
- Key details needed for cost and sign-off

*Written*

- Outline specifications
- Detailed code checklist
- Draft finish schedule and furniture list

*Presentation*

- Material and finish sample boards keyed to a plan
- Furniture photos with actual fabric samples; product data notebook
- Models or mockups if contracted

*Cost*

- Revised statement of probable cost (parameter method, allowances)

**Closes when:** The client approves DD in writing. This is the last chance to make changes without hurting cost and schedule.

**Exam notes**

- At the DD presentation exact plan dimensions are set, products and materials selected, furniture finalized, and most colors and finishes chosen.
- Real samples beat renderings for finishes; boards are often made room by room, with samples sized in proportion to how much of each material is used.

### 1.4 Construction Documents (CD)

*AIA B152: contract documents* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 8, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27

**Purpose:** Produce the legal documents the project is priced, permitted and built from: drawings, specifications and contract forms, plus separate FF&E documents.

**What you do**

- Draw the construction set in standard order: title and index, plans, reflected ceiling plans, elevations, details, then consultants' drawings (S, M, P, E, FP).
- Write the specifications in a project manual: MasterFormat divisions, three-part sections.
- Prepare FF&E documents separately from construction documents, as AIA B152 requires.
- Coordinate the consultants: regular meetings, exchanged progress prints, written notice of every change, and a check of the full set before issue.
- Make sure the set shows what the building department needs: egress, occupant loads, rated assemblies, exit signs, extinguishers, location plan.
- Cross-check drawings and specs: the same terms in both, sizes only on the drawings, quality and installation only in the specs.
- Tell the owner about cost changes from scope changes; prepare the detailed estimate.

**What gets produced**

*Drawings*

- Title/index sheet with project and code data
- Demolition plan (remodels)
- Construction (partition) plan
- Reflected ceiling plan
- Power / telephone / data plan
- Finish plan and schedule
- Furniture plan
- Interior elevations, sections and details
- Door, hardware, finish, millwork and equipment schedules
- Consultants' drawings (S, M, P, E, FP)

*Project manual*

- Bidding requirements (not part of the contract)
- Contract forms: agreement, bonds, insurance certificates
- General and supplementary conditions
- Technical specifications, Divisions 01–49 as needed

*FF&E*

- Furniture specifications and schedule
- Custom furniture and systems furniture drawings

*Cost*

- Detailed estimate; allowances, alternates and unit prices defined

**Closes when:** The owner approves the final set, which is issued for bidding or negotiation and for permit.

**Exam notes**

- When drawings and specifications conflict, courts have generally held the specifications take precedence.
- Numbering: discipline letter + group.sheet (A1.1, A2.1…) lets sheets be added without renumbering; high-rise projects prefix the floor (54A1.3 or A54-1.3).
- Bidding requirements are bound into the project manual for convenience but are not contract documents.

### 1.5 Bidding & Negotiation (BN)

*Tendering (Canada)* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 8, 18, 27, 30, 32

**Purpose:** Get a firm price and signed contracts for construction and FF&E. The designer assists; the owner decides and awards.

**What you do**

- Help the owner assemble bidding documents, procurement forms, conditions and the forms of agreement.
- Advertise publicly (public work) or invite prequalified bidders; or negotiate with one selected contractor.
- Hold a prebid conference if useful; answer every question by addendum to all bidders.
- Review substitution requests (under AIA A701, due at least 10 days before bid opening) and approve them by addendum.
- Issue the last addendum no later than 4 days before bid opening.
- Help evaluate bids: base bid, alternates, unit prices, subcontractor lists, qualifications.
- Help prepare contracts for interior construction and for FF&E, and assist with government approvals.

**What gets produced**

*Documents*

- Bid package: drawings, project manual, addenda
- Addenda
- Bid form: base bid, alternates, unit prices, time, acknowledged addenda
- Bid security (about 5% of the bid)
- Performance bond and labor & material payment bond requirements
- Bid tabulation and recommendation
- Owner–contractor agreement; owner–vendor FF&E agreement

**Closes when:** Contract award and signed agreements. The contractor then applies for the building permit.

**Exam notes**

- If the low bid exceeds the budget the owner can approve a higher budget, rebid, abandon the project, or — only with a written fixed limit of cost — have the designer revise the documents at no charge.
- Changes before award are made by addendum; after award by change order.
- Alternates give a flexible way to cut or add scope if bids come in high.

### 1.6 Contract Administration (CA)

*Construction administration · installation* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 26, 27, 31, 32

**Purpose:** Represent the owner during construction and FF&E installation: observe the work, interpret the documents, review submittals, certify payments and manage changes.

**What you do**

- Visit the site as needed to know the progress and quality of the work (not exhaustive inspections) and write field reports.
- Review shop drawings, product data and samples for conformance with the design intent only; keep a submittal log.
- Answer requests for information; interpret the documents impartially. Aesthetic decisions consistent with the documents are final.
- Process changes: minor changes in the work, construction change directives, change orders.
- Review applications for payment and certify the amounts due (retainage is typically 10%).
- Reject nonconforming work, require tests and inspections, and recommend that the owner stop work if needed.
- Coordinate FF&E: purchase orders, acknowledgments, delivery schedules, installation.
- Keep complete records: correspondence, meeting notes, logs.

**What gets produced**

*Documents*

- Field reports
- Submittal reviews and submittal log
- RFI responses
- Minor changes, construction change directives, change orders
- Certificates for payment
- Purchase orders and order tracking (FF&E)
- Meeting minutes and project records

**Closes when:** The work is ready for the substantial-completion inspection.

**Exam notes**

- The designer is not responsible for construction means, methods, sequences, job-site safety, or the contractor's errors.
- Claims must be made within 21 days; the designer takes preliminary action within 10 days; mediation comes before arbitration.
- Shop drawings are not a way to change the design; a change made there entitles the contractor to a change order.
- Under the AIA FF&E forms the owner, not the designer, inspects and accepts deliveries unless that is added as a service.

### 1.7 Closeout & Post-occupancy (CO)

*Substantial completion · move-in · POE* · NCIDQ RM Chs. 27, 28, 31

**Purpose:** Finish the work, hand over the documents the owner needs to run the space, and learn from the project.

**What you do**

- The contractor lists items still to be completed; the designer inspects and prepares the punch list.
- Issue the certificate of substantial completion (warranties usually start that day; in many states so does the statute of limitations).
- Process the final application and final certificate for payment; retainage is released with consent of surety.
- Collect warranties, maintenance contracts, operating instructions, inspection certificates, record drawings, the certificate of occupancy, extra stock and keys.
- Help the client through move-in with shake-down meetings and finish-care instructions.
- Make follow-up visits at six months and one year; offer a post-occupancy evaluation at three to six months.

**What gets produced**

*Documents*

- Punch list
- Certificate of substantial completion
- Final certificate for payment
- Warranties, maintenance contracts, O&M manuals, cleaning procedures
- Record drawings
- Certificate of occupancy (from the building department)
- Post-occupancy evaluation report
- Sustainability certification submission

**Closes when:** Final payment. The designer's formal services can end with the final certificate for payment.

**Exam notes**

- A POE asks: was the program met, is the image right, are sizes, adjacencies, flexibility, finishes, furniture, lighting, HVAC, acoustics and power adequate, and are users and owner satisfied?
- A POE is an additional service; adding it to the original agreement (AIA G802) keeps the statute of limitations running from substantial completion.
- In Canada the punch list is called the deficiency list.

**How the cost estimate sharpens:** area × unit cost in programming → statement of probable cost in SD → parameter method in DD → detailed quantities in CD → a firm price at bidding.

## 2. Worked example: Studio Lumen

A lighting manufacturer's local rep firm is leasing a 60 × 36 ft suite on the 3rd floor of a multi-tenant office building. They need a showroom to demonstrate fixtures, a reception area, a conference room, two private offices, and support space. The north wall is glass; the public corridor and elevator lobby are to the south. *Teaching illustration, not a real project. ### 2.2 Schematic Design

#### SK-1 · Concept diagram

**What it shows**

- Constraints from the base plan: columns, glass to the north, entry and elevator lobby to the south, existing plumbing
- Zoning idea: public showroom and reception in the east bay, staff along the glass, support near the plumbing
- A circulation spine that ends at a second exit, so there is no dead end

**Not on it yet**

- No rooms or walls

Zones: **public** (showroom + reception, east bay), **staff** (offices + conference on the north glass), **support** (break, toilet, storage near the existing plumbing). A circulation spine runs from the entry west to a second exit. Note: make the freestanding showroom column a display.

#### SK-2 · Block diagram

**What it shows**

- Bubbles turned into blocks scaled to the program areas and laid on the base plan
- Adjacencies kept: reception ↔ showroom ↔ workroom
- Circulation shown as its own band

**Not on it yet**

- Blocks are approximate; no walls or doors

#### SK-3 · Schematic plan

**What it shows**

### 2.3 Design Development

#### A1.1 · DD floor plan

**What it shows**

- Double-line partitions at true thickness, now fixed in place

- Partition tags at the corridor walls; furniture, fixtures and the custom reception desk

- Not every partition dimensioned or tagged

### 3.1 General, code & life safety

#### A0000 · Cover, Sheet Index & Project Data (NEW)

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

- Cartoon set: a mock-up list of the sheets the project will need, used to plan scope, fee and staffing.
- Preliminary project data: building, floor, gross area, occupancy group, and whether the building is sprinklered.

**Design Development**

- Draft sheet index matching the set as it develops.
- Project data refined: occupancy, construction type, area, preliminary occupant load.
- Design team and consultants listed.

**Construction Documents**

- Project title (and a project graphic on large jobs) and a complete sheet index for every discipline.
- Project data the building department wants on the first sheet: names and addresses of the design professionals, street address or legal description, codes used, area of the space, construction type and occupancy group(s), occupant load calculations, sprinklered or not, and the valuation of the work.
- Location or key plan showing where the space sits in the floor or building.
- General notes that apply to the whole job; abbreviations and symbols if they aren't on their own sheet.
- On small projects all of this can go on the first sheet of the set, usually the floor plan.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 8, 23, 24)

- The building department's list of required information is in Ch. 8: location plan, all means of egress, occupant loads, rated partitions and doors with details, exit signs and extinguishers, consultants' drawings, and specifications with test standards.
- Every title block carries the designer, consultants, project title and address, owner (if wanted), project number, a 2″ × 2″ stamp space, a revisions column (room for 10+), sheet title, sheet number (large, lower right), drawn by and checked by; optionally a client approval line, copyright notice, key plan, scale, north arrow and keynotes.
- Standard sheet sizes come from the architectural, ANSI or ISO series; U.S. federal projects require ANSI sizes.

#### A0001 · General Notes, Symbols & Abbreviations

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Include your office's standard general notes / symbols / abbreviations template sheet.

**Construction Documents**

- Update the standard template sheet to reflect this project's specific additions and deletions.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 23)

- Reference symbols: an elevation mark (drawing number over sheet number), a section mark (the drawing is a view at 90° to the cut), and a detail mark that circles part of a drawing, like a magnifying glass (same plane, larger scale). Each drawing gets a unique number plus the sheet it is on, e.g. 6/A5.
- Keynotes are numbered notes listed in the title block or on the sheet and referenced in the drawing field; the numbers can match specification section numbers.
- Know the common material, architectural, electrical and HVAC symbols. The Practicum may use its own symbol set.

#### A0002 · Mounting Heights

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Include your office's standard mounting heights template sheet.

**Construction Documents**

- Update the standard template sheet to reflect this project's specific additions and deletions.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 6, 9)

- Controls such as switches and thermostats go within accessible reach ranges: 15″ minimum and 48″ maximum above the floor for an unobstructed forward or side reach (2010 ADA Standards).
- Grab bars, accessories, drinking fountains, telephones, signage and counters each have their own heights; the detailed accessibility check is a DD task.

#### A0003 · Code Analysis & Life Safety Plan (NEW)

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

- Preliminary code checklist (often started in programming): occupancy classification, gross area, number of exits, exit separation, maximum travel distance, common path of egress travel, minimum exit width, dead-end corridor limits, accessibility items that affect planning, mixed-occupancy separations, corridor construction, door and glazing requirements, ramps and stairs.
- Check the basic exiting of each space-plan option: number and location of exits, separation, travel distance, common path, dead ends, door swings.
- Flag code items with big cost impact now, such as fire-rated glazing instead of gypsum board partitions.

**Design Development**

- Detailed code checklist: fire-rated corridors and partitions, shaft and occupancy separations, ratings required by construction type, stair and ramp details, safety and fire-rated glazing, door ratings and hardware, flame-spread ratings of finishes, penetrations of rated assemblies, limits on plastics.
- Life-safety devices located with the consultants: exit signs, fire extinguishers, emergency lighting, sprinklers, smoke detectors, visual and audible alarms.
- Detailed accessibility check: route surfaces and thresholds, toilet rooms and accessories, reach ranges, detectable warnings, signage, required seating.
- Occupant load calculated for each room and for the whole space.

**Construction Documents**

- Life-safety (egress) plan showing every portion of the means of egress and the exit paths; common path and travel distance where they need to be shown.
- Occupant load for every room or space and for each floor.
- Fire-rated partitions and doors with their ratings, keyed to construction details.
- Exit sign and fire extinguisher locations.
- Code summary: codes and editions used, occupancy group(s), construction type, sprinklered or not, area, occupant load.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 6, 8, 28)

- Occupant load = floor area ÷ the code's load factor. Most business areas use 150 sf gross per person; concentrated business uses (call centers, trading floors) use 50.
- The contractor usually submits the documents for permit, but the interior designer is responsible for showing code compliance in them, and plan-review corrections usually come back to the designer.
- Engineers are responsible for code compliance in their own sealed drawings; the designer, as prime consultant, is still liable to the owner for their work.

### 3.2 Interior plans

#### A01xx · Demolition Plans (NEW)

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

- Field-measured base plan of existing conditions, with photos of views, lighting and any finishes that might be reused.
- Existing construction worth keeping identified; reusing partitions, doors and finishes reduces waste and new material.

**Design Development**

- Draft demolition scope: existing partitions, doors, ceilings, finishes and equipment to remain, to be removed, or to be salvaged and relocated.
- Items consultants will remove or relocate (diffusers, lights, plumbing) coordinated.

**Construction Documents**

- Demolition plan: existing construction to remain shown solid, construction to be removed shown dashed, with keyed demolition notes.
- Items to be salvaged, protected or relocated, cross-referenced to the door schedule and the new plans.
- Patch-and-repair notes where construction is removed.
- If demolition is minor, show it dashed on the construction plan instead of on a separate sheet.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 6, 23, 24)

- A contractor needs some form of demolition plan before partitions can be removed on a remodeling project.
- Existing base-building drawings may not show finishes, a previous tenant's equipment or hardware types, so verify them in the field. Measuring can be done by hand, by photogrammetry or by laser scanning.
- The manual notes LEED credit for keeping 40% (and more for 60%) of existing non-shell elements such as walls, flooring, ceilings and doors.

#### A01xx · Floor / Construction (Partition) Plans

**By:** Interior designer / architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/16"=1'-0" [1:200] overall · 1/8"=1'-0" [1:100] or 1/4"=1'-0" [1:50] for interiors

**Schematic Design**

- Building enclosure and interior partitions. Show doors, generic wall thickness; no material indication. Differentiate opaque vs. transparent walls.
- Overall building dimensions.
- Relevant interface and extent of existing buildings. Differentiate new, existing, and demolition. Indicate phasing elements.
- Room, wing and department names if appropriate.
- Major mechanical room spaces (M/P/F/E/T).
- Accessibility requirements (interior, exterior), new and existing; clearly define egress vertically and horizontally.
- Structural grids (existing and new) with labels and dimensions — based on input from the structural engineer.
- Indicate lateral load system(s) locations and types.
- Reference symbols for building elevations and sections. Indicate datum reference relative to elevation 100'-0" (100m) for the Level 01 floor plan.
- Stairs, ramps, elevators, escalators, multi-level spaces and elements, major mechanical/electrical shafts, areaways and chases, appropriately sized.
- Major equipment, furnishings or other fixed space-defining elements — millwork, casework, seating, any fixed/non-fixed elements that impact space planning.
- Overhangs, projections, canopies, skylights, and light wells shown dashed when above.
- Key plans as appropriate for the project.

**Design Development**

- Update Schematic Design information.
- Room numbers.
- Critical dimensions — exterior openings, corridors, stairs, major shafts, toilet rooms, design and accessible elements. Tie all elements to the structural grid.
- Significant building M/P/F/E/T equipment.
- Reference symbols for match lines, building elevations, building sections, wall sections and enlarged plans.
- Equipment, casework, millwork, wardrobes, display cases, architectural features, plumbing fixtures, lockers, benches, toilet compartments.
- Owner- and contractor-furnished equipment. Installer designated.
- Doors and borrowed lights, with numbers assigned.
- Actual partition types shown to scale widths. Insert partition tags at corridors and fire-rated walls. Define movable partitions. Delineate exterior wall assemblies.
- Floor slopes, drains and level offsets.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Label exterior openings (windows, curtainwall, storefronts, louvers). Tag all remaining partitions.
- Special/custom architectural elements, unique and atypical partitions.
- Reference symbols for details. Additional reference symbols for building elevations and sections, wall sections, plan details, and interior elevations.
- Dimensionally locate all interior and exterior attached building features and partitions. Highly detailed features go on enlarged plans — include references for all.
- Control/expansion joints. Dimensionally locate with detail references.
- Areaways, pits, trenches, sumps, gratings, etc. Dimensionally locate with detail references.
- Recessed and surface-mounted equipment (fire extinguisher & hose cabinets, plumbing fixtures), pneumatic tube locations, other mechanical/electrical equipment.
- Chase and furred-out enclosures, access doors.
- HVAC cabinets (convectors, radiation, fan coils, and air devices).
- Floor elevation changes, depressed floor areas for showers, entrance & traffic mats, terrazzo, tile, etc. (verify accessibility).
- Handrails/guardrails at floor openings and floor transitions.
- Lead shielding indications.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

- Required on every project, whatever its size. The view is cut about 4 ft (1.2 m) above the floor.
- Shows walls, dimensions, existing construction to remain, references to elevations and details, room names and numbers, floor material indications, millwork, plumbing fixtures, built-ins, stairs, special equipment and notes.
- Drawn at 1/8″ (1:100) or 1/4″ (1:50); very complex areas get large-scale plans at 1/2″ (1:25). All other plans in the set should match the construction plan's scale.
- Midsize and large projects split the information over several plans of the same area: demolition, construction, finish, power/telephone and furniture.

**By:** Interior designer / architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/4"=1'-0" [1:50] or 1/2"=1'-0" [1:25]

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Enlarged plans at 1/4"=1'-0" (1:50) with layout of typical/repetitive spaces (e.g. patient rooms, classrooms, toilet rooms).

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

### 3.3 Elevations, sections & details

#### A06xx · Interior Elevations

**By:** Interior designer · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/4"=1'-0" [1:50] simple walls · 3/8" or 1/2"=1'-0" complex

> Reorganized: the original checklist listed the DD items above under the partition and door schedule sheet, and three elevator items in this sheet's CD column. They now sit where they belong; the elevator items are under Vertical Circulation.

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Elevations or three-dimensional drawings sufficient to describe the character of the design (the NCIDQ manual's DD list).
- Floor level symbols, column grid lines labeled for multi-level elevations or unique conditions that define spaces.
- Dimensions as known (don't repeat those shown elsewhere, including on the Mounting Heights sheets).
- Equipment, accessories, specialties located (tackboards, chalkboards, shelving, bumper rails, corner guards, handrails, lockers, benches, millwork, casework, fixtures, etc.).
- Areas of special architectural design interest or complexity.
- Casework/millwork elevations.
- Detail references to complex/special details. Item legends.
- Elevations of typical and repetitive spaces that provide information not identified elsewhere (finish schedule, opening schedule, typical mounting heights, etc.).

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Section cut and detail references, labels for each space, up/down indicators.
- Configuration and finish of each wall surface: openings, built-ins, materials and finishes keyed to the legend.
- Vertical dimensions, plus horizontal dimensions for wall panels or cabinet runs that won't fit on the plan.
- Switches, thermostats and other wall-mounted equipment located.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

- A straight-on view of a vertical surface, perpendicular to it, so nothing is distorted; curved or angled surfaces are projected onto the flat plane.
- Strictly an elevation is a section: only the outline of the cut floor, walls and ceiling is drawn, because their construction is detailed elsewhere.
- Scales: 1/4″ (1:50) for simple wall planes, 3/8″ (1:40) or 1/2″ (1:25) for complex surfaces.
- Referenced from plans with elevation marks: drawing number on top, sheet number below; one mark can point to several walls.

**Real example (public domain):** [Farnsworth House — Bathroom elevations & wardrobe plans (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=7)

#### A08xx · Interior Sections & Details

**By:** Interior designer · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1", 1-1/2" or 3"=1'-0" · half or full size for small parts

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Details of sufficient resolution for complex/special conditions, and as necessary to obtain client sign-off and establish cost estimates.
- E-spec keynotes.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Details to include: wall protection, ceilings, floor transitions, plan/section conditions. Show and dimension changes in geometry and transitions between materials/products.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 21, 24)

- A section shows what construction would look like if cut straight through; most interior 'details' are large-scale sections of small pieces of construction. A detail can also be a large-scale plan, elevation or isometric.
- Typical subjects: wall types, doors, glazing, ceilings, millwork, stairs, flooring, special construction. Competitively bid projects need complete details; negotiated projects may get by with fewer.
- Scales: 1″ (1:10), 1-1/2″ (1:8), 3″ (1:4); half or full size for very small, complex parts.
- A detail shows the materials cut and also what lies beyond the cut, like a small elevation. Good details also consider constructability, tolerances, durability and code.

**Real example (public domain):** [Farnsworth House — Stair section & wardrobe details (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=7)

#### A09xx · Casework & Millwork

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Use your office's standard casework/millwork template sheet and update it to reflect project-specific additions and deletions.
- Millwork details of sufficient resolution for complex/special conditions, and as necessary to obtain client sign-off and establish cost estimates.
- E-spec keynotes.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Millwork details to include section and plan conditions. Show and dimension changes in geometry and transitions between materials/products.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 17, 25, 27)

- DD documents include sketches of special built-in cabinetry and custom furniture; CD adds elevations, sections and details with materials, hardware and dimensions.
- Custom architectural woodwork is specified in Division 06; manufactured casework belongs in Division 12.
- Woodwork quality is set by referencing industry standards and grades (economy, custom, premium) in the specifications rather than by notes on the drawings.

**Real example (public domain):** [Farnsworth House — Wardrobe plans & details (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=7)

### 3.4 Schedules, types & legends

#### A10xx · Partition Types, Door/Frame/Opening Types & Schedules

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Use your office's standard partition-types template sheet, unedited.
- Use your office's standard opening-schedule template. For doors and borrowed lights, indicate door or light number, type, and material. Include project-specific frame and door types; delete others from the standard sheet.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Edit the standard partition-types sheet to reflect project-specific conditions (unique partition types), STC ratings, insulation considerations.
- Complete the standard opening-schedule sheet.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 11, 24, 25)

- A schedule lists unique items (one row each) against attributes they all share, which packs a lot of information into little space.
- Door schedule columns: door number, door type, material, width, height, thickness, frame type and material, head/jamb/sill detail references, fire rating, hardware group, remarks.
- Hardware groups are defined in the specifications and referenced from the door schedule.
- Other common interior schedules: room finish, kitchen equipment, millwork and hardware.

#### A12xx · Finish Plans, Legend & Finish Schedule

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Finish schedule or parametric plans defining materials, wall protection and other finish treatments — team decision on which method best documents the work.
- Identify areas with depressed/raised slabs, decks.
- Finish legends, symbols, tags, material designations/patterns.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Color, texture, type and pattern variations for selected products and materials. Dimensions, notes, references.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

- A room finish schedule lists each room with its floor, base, each wall and the ceiling (material, color, height). It works for simple projects with one finish per wall.
- When walls carry several finishes, a finish plan is clearer: each finish gets a code (WP1, WP2…) keyed to a legend giving manufacturer, catalog number and color.
- Installation requirements belong in the specifications, not on the finish plan.
- Put the finish schedule on the same sheet as the floor plan when there is no separate finish plan.

#### A11xx · Equipment

**By:** Interior designer

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Typically incorporate equipment information into the Floor Plans or Enlarged Plans sheets. Provide separate equipment plans only if required by contract or for clarity in the document set.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 25)

- Know where items are specified: Division 10 Specialties (small items installed in multiples — lockers, toilet accessories, corner guards, signage), Division 11 Equipment (larger, costlier items — AV, food service, library, medical), Division 12 Furnishings (furniture, manufactured casework, window treatments).
- Mark owner-furnished vs. contractor-furnished items and who installs them.

#### A15xx · Signage & Wayfinding (NEW)

**By:** Interior designer / graphics consultant

**Schematic Design**

- Wayfinding strategy as part of the concept: entries, decision points, room identification.

**Design Development**

- Sign types and locations drafted; required code signage identified (exits, accessible routes and entrances, room identification, areas of refuge).

**Construction Documents**

- Signage location plan with sign-type tags; sign-type elevations and a schedule of message, size, materials and mounting.
- Accessible room-identification signs with raised characters and braille, mounted on the latch side of the door with the tactile text between 48″ and 60″ above the floor (measured to the baselines).
- Exit and directional signs coordinated with the life-safety plan.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 9, 11, 27)

- AIA B152 lists design and selection of graphics and signage as an additional service, so confirm it is in the scope.
- Accessible signage requirements (character height and style, contrast, braille, mounting location) are part of the detailed DD accessibility review.

### 3.5 Consultants' drawings

#### S-series · Structural Drawings (NEW)

**By:** Structural engineer

**Schematic Design**

- Floor capacity confirmed for heavy loads (high-density files, libraries, thick-set stone floors) and any structural changes identified, such as new floor openings or stairs.
- Engineer's input on the existing structural grid and constraints.

**Design Development**

- Preliminary framing for new openings, stairs and supports for heavy or ceiling-hung elements.
- Member sizes shared so interior details leave room for construction, clearances, tolerances and finishes.

**Construction Documents**

- Framing plans (if needed), major structural sections, detail sections and connection details, sealed by the structural engineer.
- Placed after the interior drawings and before the mechanical drawings in the set.
- Interior drawings show only the outline of structural walls and framing; elevations of beam tops, structural walls and floors appear on both sets.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 12, 23, 24, 28)

- A structural engineer must be retained for any structural modification: frame, floors or loadbearing walls.
- The interior designer coordinates exact member sizes into interior details.

#### M / P-series · Mechanical (HVAC) & Plumbing Drawings (NEW)

**By:** Mechanical engineer

**Schematic Design**

- Early system assumptions from the engineer (zoning, equipment space, shafts) so the plan doesn't develop around an unworkable scheme.
- Plumbing fixtures kept close to existing plumbing lines and stacks, because drain lines must slope.

**Design Development**

- Preliminary duct mains and the diffuser and grille layout coordinated on the designer's RCP; plumbing fixture locations and counts.
- Equipment rooms and clearances sized.

**Construction Documents**

- Mechanical equipment locations; ductwork, piping, fixtures and other major components; plumbing isometrics; mechanical room details; connection and support details; equipment schedules.
- The interior set shows mechanical and plumbing items only where they meet other construction: grilles, registers and sprinkler heads on the RCP, plumbing fixtures on plans, ducts and pipes in sections and details.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 13, 23, 24, 28)

- Mechanical engineers usually design plumbing and the sprinkler system too, although a fire protection consultant may be used.
- Coordination tools: regular meetings, exchanged progress prints, written notice of every change, and a full-set check before issue.

#### E-series · Electrical Drawings (NEW)

**By:** Electrical engineer

**Schematic Design**

- Power, data and lighting needs established; preliminary lighting concept developed with the designer or a lighting designer.

**Design Development**

- Designer's furniture, outlet and lighting layout issued to the engineer; panel and equipment space confirmed.

**Construction Documents**

- Power, lighting and telecommunication plans; signal and security systems; one-line diagram; transformer, panel, equipment and fixture schedules.
- Exact circuiting of lights and outlets: number and size of conductors, conduit sizes, home runs to panels.
- Simple projects can show telecom and signal work on the power plan; complex projects get a plan per system.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 13, 23, 24)

- A one-line (single-line) diagram shows the electrical system from the building feed to the panels with single lines and symbols; branch circuits are not shown. Building departments require it.
- On most residential work the designer shows light fixture and switch locations schematically; the electrical contractor sizes the wire and circuits to code.

#### FP-series · Fire Protection Drawings (NEW)

**By:** Mechanical engineer / fire protection consultant

**Schematic Design**

- Whether the building is sprinklered confirmed early: it changes travel distances, rating requirements and other code allowances.

**Design Development**

- Sprinkler head layout coordinated with the ceiling design, lights and diffusers.

**Construction Documents**

- Fire protection plans completed by a mechanical engineer (or a fire protection consultant or contractor) and included in the set when a sprinkler system is required.
- Fire protection shop drawings may have to be submitted to show code conformance for the permit.
- Sprinkler heads also appear on the designer's RCP for coordination.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 8, 13, 24)

- Fire-related materials (doors, firestopping) are specified in their own divisions; Division 21 covers suppression systems.

#### T / AV / SEC · Technology, AV & Security (NEW)

**By:** Electrical engineer or specialty consultants

**Schematic Design**

- Needs identified in programming: AV in conference rooms, security and access control, telecom rooms.

**Design Development**

- Device locations coordinated: displays, projectors and screens, card readers, cameras, speakers, wireless access points.

**Construction Documents**

- Telecommunication, signal and security drawings by the electrical engineer, or by specialty consultants on larger projects (security, telecom, AV, acoustics).
- The designer's drawings show coordination items only: blocking for wall displays, ceiling projectors and their view patterns, floor boxes.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 13, 25, 28)

- Division 27 covers communications and Division 28 electronic safety and security.
- Specialty consultants the manual lists: acoustical, specifications, code, food service, audiovisual, telecommunications, security, lighting.

### 3.6 Base-building sheets (usually by the architect)

#### A0051 · Architectural Site Plan

**By:** Architect / civil engineer

**Schematic Design**

- All property lines with dimensions to building faces. Include bordering streets and rights-of-way, if not shown on civil drawings.
- Paved areas including all ingress/egress points, roads, walks/plazas, and parking. Show parking layout(s) listing total number of spaces.
- Principal landscape features including major topographical rework, significant plantings, site structures/amenities, design features.
- Important exterior features linked to the building design (views, connections, pavements, landscaping, utility items).
- Hardscape paving patterns, special paving areas, site walls, signage elements.
- Existing buildings to remain. Dimensions between proposed new and existing, if not shown on civil drawings.
- Footprint of new buildings indicating all points of entry with spot elevations. Set elevation datum relative to Level 01 at 100'-0" (100m).
- Phases or future expansion.

**Design Development**

- Update Schematic Design information.
- Enlarged plans and sections as necessary to convey design intent for site structures/amenities, landscaped areas and other design features.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Enlarged plans, sections, elevations and details for site structures/amenities, landscaped areas and other design features not shown by other disciplines.
- Coursing, patterns, jointing, and spacing requirements for surfaces and design features not shown by other disciplines.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

- The interior designer does not draw a site plan, but the exam may ask you to recognize one: a view from directly above showing the roof and the yards, walks, driveways and other features inside the property line, plus the adjacent streets.

#### A01xx · Roof Plans

**By:** Architect

**Schematic Design**

- Graphically depict and define the extents of parapet, roof edge, and gravel-stop perimeter conditions at each roof area/level.
- Roof areas at multiple floor levels: show the roof areas visible at each floor level on the corresponding plan for that level. Combined roof/floor plans should exclude lower roof plans not on that level.
- Overall Roof Plan: show the entire multilevel roof view and reference the individual roof area plans.
- Delineate all new roof work on existing buildings.
- Define and reference roofing assembly type(s) for each roof area/level.
- Roof screens — location, size and materials.

**Design Development**

- Update Schematic Design information.
- Penthouse structures, dunnage framing, structures, miscellaneous rooftop equipment.
- Critical dimensions — overall building, significant protrusions and voids from the perimeter, curves, angles. Tie all elements to the structural grid.
- Column grids (new and existing) — use letters and numbers.
- Graphically depict the tapered roof insulation layout, including saddles (crickets). Shade the saddles/crickets. Include roof slope (e.g. 1/4"/12") with arrows showing the direction of all roof slopes, including saddles/crickets.
- Roof drainage strategy. Locate roof drains, overflow drainage, scuppers, downspouts and gutters. Indicate high and low point roof elevations. Model roof slopes.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Graphically indicate all elevator overruns, helicopter landing platforms, skylights, clerestory elements, courtyards, green roof areas (intensive & extensive), major structures, and equipment.
- Graphically depict all rooftop mechanical units at approximate size using dashed lines, so the roofing and curb can still be shown.
- Include building elevation markers, building section cuts, and detail call-outs.
- Identify all locations of saddle flashing conditions and denote on drawings.
- Define extent of roof-to-wall deflection joints at the perimeter.
- Define the widths and extent of roof expansion joints, including changes in plane and roof-to-wall transitions.
- Roof and facade access: indicate roof fall prevention and window washing systems — coordinate for aesthetics and structural requirements, and avoid obstructions (e.g. MEP equipment).
- Confirm roof overflow design, roof deflection and expansion joint accommodations, facade and roof access layout.
- Verify roofing clearances between and around rooftop equipment, roof hatches, vents, drains, ductwork and other MEP systems.
- Confirm all roof areas are accessible.
- Location and type of roof walkway pathways.
- Location of guards, railings, fall protection elements.

#### A03xx · Building Elevations

**By:** Architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/16"=1'-0" [1:200] for large buildings · 1/8"=1'-0" [1:100] otherwise

**Schematic Design**

- Verify all major elevations are started.
- Differentiate vertical planes with line weights to clearly delineate building massing, exterior openings, canopies, overhangs, soffits, phasing and other key elements.
- Grade intersection at the plane of the building face.
- Major floor and roof elevations with Level 01 established at datum 100'-0" (100m).
- Column grid lines, new and existing (numbered, lettered).
- Exterior wall assemblies.
- Significant mechanical and electrical equipment, including visible rooftop equipment, chimneys, louvers.
- Roof screen systems.

**Design Development**

- Update Schematic Design information.
- In addition to 1/16" overall elevations, include 1/8" scale elevations referenced from the overall building elevations.
- Vertical and horizontal dimensions as known.
- Exterior opening types and wall assemblies with tags.
- Exterior features — flagpoles, lighting, signage, etc.
- Fenestration design — mullion layout, operable and fixed, glazing types, etc.
- Building and typical wall section reference symbols.
- Indicate window-washing systems — coordinate for aesthetics and structural requirements.
- Show all minor, hidden and enlarged elevations for areas not started in Schematic Design.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Reference all additional building and wall sections.
- 1/4" scale elevations referenced from other building elevation sheets.
- Stairs, ramps, handrails, guardrails.
- Exterior opening designations, tags, references.
- Expansion joints, contraction joints, panel boundaries, etc.
- Dimensionally locate all elements not otherwise indicated in plan or section (coordinated with structural engineering).
- Delineate all mock-up requirements if applicable to the project scope.

**Real example (public domain):** [Robie House — North, west & east elevations (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0039.sheet/?sp=6) · [Farnsworth House — West & East elevations (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=5)

#### A03xx · Building Sections

**By:** Architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/16"=1'-0" [1:200] for large buildings · 1/8"=1'-0" [1:100] otherwise

**Schematic Design**

- Longitudinal and transverse sections to explain building massing and interior volumes.
- Include existing building context.
- Generic thickness of foundations, footings, piers, caissons, structure, floors and walls. Show openings, basements, crawl spaces.
- Level symbols and elevation datum referenced to Level 01 = 100'-0" (100m) for all floor levels.
- Grade intersection with exterior walls.
- Floor slab thickness.
- Structural grids (existing and new) with labels and dimensions.
- Major room names.
- Ship-style ladders (roofs, other maintenance areas).
- Significant rooftop equipment, chimneys, louvers, required screening.
- Generic ceiling lines, bulkheads.

**Design Development**

- Update Schematic Design information.
- 1/16" or 1/8"=1'-0" scale typically used.
- Typical and non-typical wall sections.
- Vertical and horizontal utility chases & pathways, areaways, intakes, exhaust pathways.
- Vertical circulation elements (stairs, elevators — passenger & freight, ramps, escalators, dumbwaiters).
- Outlines of overhangs, canopies, roofs, projecting elements.
- Exterior openings (doors, windows, curtainwall, louvers) with numbers.
- Skylights, lightwells, areaways, courtyards.
- Building sections necessary to define building complexity. Do not show interior elevation views beyond the cut.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Show related section detail references.
- Significant interior design elements.

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Ch. 24)

- On architectural drawings 'section' usually means a cut through the whole building; on interior drawings most sections are details of small portions of construction.

**Real example (public domain):** [Robie House — South elevation & longitudinal section (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0039.sheet/?sp=5) · [Farnsworth House — Elevations & sections (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=5)

#### A04xx · Wall Sections & Exterior Details

**By:** Architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 3/8" or 1/2"=1'-0" preferred

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Typical exterior wall sections and plan details at typical wall and roof assembly conditions. Reference roof and exterior wall assemblies. Identify air/moisture barrier locations.
- Level symbols and elevation datum referenced to Level 01 = 100'-0" (100m) for all floor levels, including basement and crawl spaces.
- Typical slab edge details at curtainwall and exterior wall interfaces, and thicknesses.
- Main elements shown for each wall assembly.
- Ceilings with heights, soffits, bulkheads.
- Column grids (new and existing) — use letters and numbers.
- Vertical and horizontal dimensions tied to the structural grid and floor levels. Dimension offsets, masonry/stone coursing, transitions, and placement of design elements.
- E-spec keynotes.

**Construction Documents**

- Update Design Development information.
- Reference typical section details on wall sections (door, window, curtainwall, storefront, louver sills, heads, jambs).
- Special or custom architectural elements; references to detail enlargements.
- Major room and space names with numbers.
- Detail at each unique corner, column enclosure, head, jamb, sill, intersection, etc.

**Real example (public domain):** [Farnsworth House — Stair section details (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=7)

#### A05xx · Vertical Circulation

**By:** Architect · **Scale:** Typical scale: 1/4"=1'-0"

**Schematic Design**

*Not required this phase.*

**Design Development**

- Provide 1/4" scale plans and sections of all stairs, elevators and escalators.
- Areas of rescue assistance where applicable.
- Stair plans tied to grid lines. Show and dimension railings and guards, landings, number and size of treads, tread widths, clearances.
- Stair sections tied to floor levels. Show floor-to-floor heights, number and height of risers, clearances, railings and guards, ladders and roof hatches.
- Elevator and other vertical shafts.
- Ramps with slopes, intermediate landings.
- Hoistways, machine rooms; verify clearances and interior dimensional requirements.

**Construction Documents**

- Dimension all elements.
- Plan and section details, including stair tread/nosing construction, landing support, stringers, railing and guard configurations and support, slab edge conditions, jambs.
- Section and plan details, including 3D details required to explain complex conditions.
- Elevator/escalator plans tied to grid lines. Show shaft clearances and openings. (moved from Interior Elevations)
- Standpipes and risers. (moved from Interior Elevations)
- Tie elevator sections to floor levels. Show pits, overrides, ladders. (moved from Interior Elevations)

**From the NCIDQ manual** (Chs. 6, 11, 28)

- Interior stairs and ramps (treads, risers, handrails, guards, landings, slopes) are checked in DD against the detailed code checklist.
- Where the interior designer leads a project, local rules may require an architect for structure-related work such as fire-rated shafts, exterior walls, the life safety system and elevators.

**Real example (public domain):** [Farnsworth House — Stair section details (HABS)](https://www.loc.gov/resource/hhh.il0323.sheet/?sp=7)

*Phase names follow the AIA sequence used across U.S. practice: programming, schematic design, design development, construction documents (AIA B152 calls this phase "contract documents"), bidding or negotiation, and contract administration.
